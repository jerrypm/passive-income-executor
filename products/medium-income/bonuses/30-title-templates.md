# 30 Medium Title Templates

These are the 30 templates from Chapter 4 of the main guide, organized by hook category. Each template is a proven pattern pulled from hundreds of high-performing Medium articles — not guesswork. Every template includes a tech-niche worked example and a non-tech worked example so you can see the pattern in action across different audiences. Substitute your specifics in `[BRACKETS]`, keep it concrete, and run every candidate through the Chapter 4 length check before you publish.

---

## Usage Hooks (5 templates)

The "I use this" framing signals firsthand experience. Readers trust personal curation over generic lists because they know you've filtered out the junk. The pattern is `[N] [items] I Use [context]` — the number makes the list scannable, the first-person verb creates authority, and the context tells readers exactly who this is for.

1. `[N] [items] I Use [Daily / Every Day / In Production]`
   - Tech: "12 SwiftUI Components I Use Every Day"
   - Non-tech: "10 Productivity Apps I Use Daily as a Freelancer"

2. `[N] [items] I Use Instead of [Common Alternative]`
   - Tech: "7 VS Code Extensions I Use Instead of WebStorm"
   - Non-tech: "5 Notion Templates I Use Instead of Asana"

3. `[N] [items] I've Used Every Week for [X] Years`
   - Tech: "8 GitHub Actions I've Used Every Week for 3 Years"
   - Non-tech: "6 Habits I've Used Every Week for 5 Years to Stay Healthy"

4. `[N] [items] in My [Daily / Weekly] Workflow`
   - Tech: "9 CLI Tools in My Daily Workflow"
   - Non-tech: "7 Frameworks in My Weekly Writing Workflow"

5. `[N] [items] That Live On My [Home Screen / Bookmarks Bar / Dock]`
   - Tech: "11 Apps That Live On My Mac Dock as a Developer"
   - Non-tech: "8 Books That Live On My Bookshelf as a Coach"

## Outcome Hooks (5 templates)

The "this changed my X" framing leads with a concrete result. Readers don't care about tools or methods in the abstract — they care about what those things did. When you attach a real number or a real state change, the title becomes a promise. The pattern is `[N] [items] That [outcome]` — the outcome is the entire headline; the number is there to make it feel achievable and specific.

6. `[N] [items] That Saved Me [X Hours / X Dollars / X Months]`
   - Tech: "10 Bash Aliases That Saved Me 200 Hours This Year"
   - Non-tech: "7 Habits That Saved Me $5,000 in My First Year of Freelancing"

7. `[N] [items] That Changed My [Workflow / Career / Income]`
   - Tech: "8 Code Review Practices That Changed My Career"
   - Non-tech: "5 Reading Habits That Changed My Income"

8. `[N] [items] That Made Me $[Amount] in [Period]`
   - Tech: "6 SwiftUI Articles That Made Me $400 in 6 Months"
   - Non-tech: "4 Newsletter Posts That Made Me $1,200 in 90 Days"

9. `[N] [items] That [Doubled / Tripled] My [Output / Reads / Sales]`
   - Tech: "9 Refactoring Patterns That Doubled My Code Review Speed"
   - Non-tech: "5 Title Formulas That Tripled My Medium Reads"

10. `[N] [items] That Took Me from [Before State] to [After State]`
    - Tech: "7 Habits That Took Me from Junior to Senior in 18 Months"
    - Non-tech: "6 Daily Practices That Took Me from Anxious to Calm"

## Contrarian Hooks (5 templates)

The "you're doing it wrong" framing creates immediate tension. Most readers assume they already know the right way — a title that challenges that assumption forces a click to resolve the discomfort. Use this hook only when you genuinely disagree with common advice and have a better alternative ready. Empty contrarianism is easy to spot and hard to forgive.

11. `[N] [items] Most [audience] Get Wrong`
    - Tech: "8 SwiftUI Patterns Most iOS Developers Get Wrong"
    - Non-tech: "6 Productivity Tips Most Founders Get Wrong"

12. `[N] [Common Belief] That Are Actually Wrong`
    - Tech: "5 Code Review Best Practices That Are Actually Wrong"
    - Non-tech: "7 Time Management Rules That Are Actually Wrong"

13. `Why I Stopped [Common Practice] (And [N] Things I Do Instead)`
    - Tech: "Why I Stopped Using Mocks in iOS Tests (And 6 Things I Do Instead)"
    - Non-tech: "Why I Stopped Reading Self-Help Books (And 5 Things I Read Instead)"

14. `[N] [items] Everyone Recommends That You Should Skip`
    - Tech: "7 Architecture Patterns Everyone Recommends That You Should Skip"
    - Non-tech: "6 Marketing Tactics Everyone Recommends That You Should Skip"

15. `Stop [Common Practice]: [N] [items] That Work Better`
    - Tech: "Stop Writing Long Functions: 8 Patterns That Work Better"
    - Non-tech: "Stop Cold Emailing: 5 Outreach Methods That Work Better"

## Regret Hooks (5 templates)

The "I wish I knew this earlier" framing is among the most empathetic on this list. It positions you not as someone who knows better than the reader, but as someone who made the same mistakes and came out the other side. That honesty lowers the reader's guard. The implicit promise is: "I already paid the cost of learning this — you don't have to."

16. `[N] [items] I Wish I Knew Before [milestone]`
    - Tech: "10 Things I Wish I Knew Before My First iOS Job"
    - Non-tech: "7 Things I Wish I Knew Before Quitting My 9-to-5"

17. `[N] [items] My [Past Self / Junior Me] Should Have Done`
    - Tech: "8 Things My Junior Self Should Have Done as a Developer"
    - Non-tech: "6 Things My 25-Year-Old Self Should Have Done with Money"

18. `[N] Mistakes I Made [Doing X] (So You Don't Have To)`
    - Tech: "7 Mistakes I Made Building My First SaaS (So You Don't Have To)"
    - Non-tech: "5 Mistakes I Made Launching My Newsletter (So You Don't Have To)"

19. `[N] [items] I'd Tell My [Younger Self / Past Self] Right Now`
    - Tech: "6 Things I'd Tell My Junior-Dev Self About Code Reviews"
    - Non-tech: "8 Things I'd Tell My 30-Year-Old Self About Investing"

20. `If I Started [X] Again, I'd Do These [N] Things Differently`
    - Tech: "If I Started iOS Development Again, I'd Do These 7 Things Differently"
    - Non-tech: "If I Started Freelancing Again, I'd Do These 5 Things Differently"

## Secret Hooks (5 templates)

The "hidden / underrated" framing taps into a universal desire: the feeling of being let in on something most people miss. This hook works best when the items genuinely are obscure — buried in documentation, skipped in tutorials, or overshadowed by more popular alternatives. If the items are well-known, calling them "hidden" will feel like a bait-and-switch and you'll lose the reader's trust before they finish the intro.

21. `[N] Underrated [items]`
    - Tech: "9 Underrated Swift APIs Every iOS Dev Should Know"
    - Non-tech: "7 Underrated Books for Solo Entrepreneurs"

22. `[N] Hidden [items] [in / for] [Tool / Practice]`
    - Tech: "11 Hidden VS Code Shortcuts for Faster Development"
    - Non-tech: "6 Hidden Notion Features for Better Note-Taking"

23. `[N] [items] [Audience] Don't Talk About`
    - Tech: "8 Things Senior iOS Devs Don't Talk About"
    - Non-tech: "5 Things Successful Freelancers Don't Talk About Publicly"

24. `[N] Lesser-Known [items] That [Outcome]`
    - Tech: "7 Lesser-Known Swift Operators That Make Code Cleaner"
    - Non-tech: "6 Lesser-Known Productivity Tools That Beat Notion"

25. `[N] [items] Buried in [Documentation / Settings / Menus]`
    - Tech: "9 SwiftUI Modifiers Buried in the Documentation"
    - Non-tech: "6 macOS Settings Buried in System Preferences"

## Credibility Hooks (5 templates)

The "after X years/months" framing does two things at once: it establishes that you've put in serious time, and it signals that what follows is distilled — not raw. Readers infer that you've already filtered out the bad ideas over those years, so the list they're about to read is high-signal. The number of years matters less than the specificity. "After 5 years" is stronger than "after years of experience."

26. `After [X Years / Months] of [Activity], Here Are [N] [items] Worth Knowing`
    - Tech: "After 5 Years of iOS Development, Here Are 12 Things Worth Knowing"
    - Non-tech: "After 3 Years of Freelance Writing, Here Are 8 Things Worth Knowing"

27. `[X] Years In: [N] [items] That Still [Hold Up / Matter / Work]`
    - Tech: "8 Years In: 10 SwiftUI Patterns That Still Hold Up"
    - Non-tech: "5 Years In: 7 Marketing Tactics That Still Work"

28. `What [X] Years of [Activity] Taught Me About [N] [items]`
    - Tech: "What 5 Years of iOS Work Taught Me About 8 Code Smells"
    - Non-tech: "What 10 Years of Coaching Taught Me About 6 Habits That Stick"

29. `[N] [items] I've [Earned / Learned / Refined] Over [X] Years`
    - Tech: "11 Architecture Decisions I've Refined Over 7 Years"
    - Non-tech: "8 Negotiation Tactics I've Earned Over 12 Years of Sales"

30. `[Profession] for [X] Years: My [N] [items] for [audience]`
    - Tech: "iOS Developer for 7 Years: My 10 Tips for Junior Devs"
    - Non-tech: "Freelance Designer for 8 Years: My 6 Rules for Pricing"

## How to Use a Template

These templates are starting points, not finished titles. A template gives you the skeleton; your specifics are what make it worth clicking.

**Step 1 — Pick a hook category that matches your story angle.**
Ask yourself what kind of article you're actually writing before you pick a template. Usage hooks fit "I curated a list from personal experience." Outcome hooks fit "I got a result and I can prove it." Contrarian hooks fit "I disagree with what most people say, and here's why." Regret hooks fit "I learned this the hard way so you don't have to." Secret hooks fit "I found something most people overlook." Credibility hooks fit "I've spent years on this and I want to share what I know." Picking the wrong category means the title will feel off even if the variables are right.

**Step 2 — Substitute the variables in `[BRACKETS]` with your specifics.**
Vague variables produce vague titles. "Things" should become "SwiftUI components" or "morning habits" or "cold outreach techniques." "X dollars" should become "$67" or "$2,400" — use a real number if you have it. "X years" should be the actual count, not a round number you rounded up to sound more credible. The more concrete the substitution, the stronger the title. Readers scan headlines in milliseconds and concrete numbers stop the scroll where vague words do not.

**Step 3 — Run the title through Chapter 4's length check.**
Every candidate title should be 6 to 12 words long and 50 to 70 characters total. Discard any title that fails either check. Too short and it lacks context; too long and it gets cut off in feeds and loses its punch. If you're over the limit, cut the least informative word first — usually an adjective or a filler phrase like "as a developer" that can be implied from context.
